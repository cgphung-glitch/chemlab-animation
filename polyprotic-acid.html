Acid speciation

Fraction of species, α, versus pH

Drag across the plot to zoom into a pH range. Hover or tap to read values.

Reference: derivation of the speciation equations

Setup

Let C be the total (analytical) concentration of all forms of the acid, and define the fraction of species i as αi = [species i] / C. The two assumptions are that concentrations stand in for activities and that no other reactions (such as metal binding) involve the acid. Under these assumptions α depends only on [H⁺] and the Ka values, and not on C. The plot uses pH = −log[H⁺] and pKa = −log Ka.

Diprotic acid, H₂A

Acid–base equilibria:

H₂A ⇌ H⁺ + HA⁻    Ka1 = [H⁺][HA⁻] / [H₂A]
HA⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + A²⁻    Ka2 = [H⁺][A²⁻] / [HA⁻]

Mass balance:

C = [H₂A] + [HA⁻] + [A²⁻]

Rearrange each equilibrium expression to write every species in terms of [H₂A]:

[HA⁻] = Ka1[H₂A] / [H⁺]
[A²⁻] = Ka1Ka2[H₂A] / [H⁺]²

Substitute into the mass balance and multiply through by [H⁺]²:

C = [H₂A] ( 1 + Ka1/[H⁺] + Ka1Ka2/[H⁺]² )
[H₂A] / C = [H⁺]² / D,   D = [H⁺]² + Ka1[H⁺] + Ka1Ka2

Dividing each species by C gives:

α₀ (H₂A) = [H⁺]² / D
α₁ (HA⁻) = Ka1[H⁺] / D
α₂ (A²⁻) = Ka1Ka2 / D

Triprotic acid, H₃A

A third equilibrium, HA²⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + A³⁻ with Ka3 = [H⁺][A³⁻] / [HA²⁻], is added, and the mass balance becomes C = [H₃A] + [H₂A⁻] + [HA²⁻] + [A³⁻]. The same steps (write each species in terms of [H₃A], substitute, multiply through by [H⁺]³) give:

D = [H⁺]³ + Ka1[H⁺]² + Ka1Ka2[H⁺] + Ka1Ka2Ka3
α₀ (H₃A) = [H⁺]³ / D
α₁ (H₂A⁻) = Ka1[H⁺]² / D
α₂ (HA²⁻) = Ka1Ka2[H⁺] / D
α₃ (A³⁻) = Ka1Ka2Ka3 / D

General pattern

For an acid with n ionizable protons, the denominator is the sum of n + 1 terms. The term for the species that has lost i protons is Ka1⋯Kai[H⁺]n−i, with the empty product equal to 1 when i = 0. Each αi is that term divided by D, so Σαi = 1 at every pH. This is the calculation the app performs.

pH calculator (charge balance)

For a total concentration C of the acid or of one of its sodium salts, [Na⁺] = iC, where i is the number of protons already removed from the parent acid (0 for the acid itself, 1 for NaHA, 2 for Na₂A, and so on). The solution must be electrically neutral:

[H⁺] + iC = Kw / [H⁺] + C Σ j αj([H⁺])

Here j is the number of protons lost by species j (equal to its negative charge), and Kw = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C. The left side minus the right side decreases steadily as pH rises, so there is exactly one root. The app finds it by bisection between pH −2 and 16, then reports [H⁺], pOH and each species concentration as αjC. No shortcuts such as ignoring water or assuming a small extent of ionization are used.

Features you can check on the plot

  • At pH = pKa1, [H₂A] = [HA⁻] exactly, because Ka1 = [H⁺][HA⁻]/[H₂A]. The same holds at each later pKa for the neighboring pair. This is why the dashed pKa lines pass through the crossings of neighboring curves when the pKa values are well separated.
  • When successive pKa values are close (for example citric acid, pKa1 = 3.13 and pKa2 = 4.76), the intermediate species never reaches α = 1 and the neighboring forms overlap.
  • At very low pH, α₀ → 1. At very high pH, the fully deprotonated form → 1.

Further reading

This derivation is the standard treatment of polyprotic systems in analytical chemistry texts, for example in the chapters on polyprotic acids and bases in Harris, Quantitative Chemical Analysis, and in Skoog et al., Fundamentals of Analytical Chemistry.
